Find the submissions we did a few years ago where we argued that a notice of delay under clause 34.1 was not a condition precedent to an extension of time.

Two of the firm’s submissions make that argument:

  1. Tamsley Glass v Orrindale Built (adjudication application, QLD, 2023). Clause 34.1 imposed an obligation to give notice within 7 days, but did not make the extension conditional on it; clause 34.3, by contrast, used express words of time bar.p 14
  2. Brenlock Civil v Ostrova Projects (further submissions, QLD, 2024). The Superintendent’s power to extend time “whether or not the Contractor has given notice” is inconsistent with notice being a condition precedent.p 6

Tamsley Glass Pty Ltd v Orrindale Built Pty LtdClaimant’s submissions
51.The Respondent has withheld $54,600.00 as liquidated damages for 26 days of delay to Practical Completion (Payment Schedule, item 3; Tab 4). Its only answer to the Claimant’s claim for an extension of time for the façade delay is that notice of that delay was given late.
E.THE NOTICE PROVISION IS NOT A CONDITION PRECEDENT
52.The Respondent contends that, because the Claimant gave notice of the façade delay 11 days after it became aware of it, rather than within the 7 days required by clause 34.1 of the Subcontract, the Claimant is not entitled to any extension of time for that delay (Payment Schedule, item 3; Respondent’s letter of 2 June 2023, Tab 31).
53.That contention should be rejected. Clause 34.1 relevantly provides:
“The Subcontractor must, within 7 days after becoming aware of any delay to the progress of the Subcontract Works, give the Contractor written notice of the delay and its cause.”
54.Clause 34.1 imposes an obligation to give notice. It does not provide that compliance is a condition of the entitlement to an extension of time, or that the entitlement is lost if notice is given late. Where the parties intended that consequence, they said so in terms: clause 34.3 provides that a claim for delay costs “shall be barred” unless made within 28 days.
55.The contrast is telling. In the same clause the parties chose express words of forfeiture for delay costs, and did not use them for extensions of time. A provision that would deprive a party of an accrued entitlement is construed strictly, and is not extended by implication beyond its terms.
56.Further, clause 34.5 empowers the Contractor to extend the Date for Practical Completion “at any time and whether or not the Subcontractor has claimed an extension”. That power sits uneasily with notice being a condition precedent and, where the Contractor itself caused the delay, it must be exercised honestly and fairly: Peninsula Balmain Pty Ltd v Abigroup Contractors Pty Ltd [2002] NSWCA 211.
57.In any event the late notice caused the Respondent no prejudice. Its own site instruction of 14 March 2023 records the façade delay four days after it began (Davies SD at [27]; Tab 22).
58.The Claimant is therefore entitled to the extension of 26 days claimed, and the Respondent is not entitled to the liquidated damages of $54,600.00 it has withheld.

Brenlock Civil Pty Ltd v Ostrova Projects Pty LtdClaimant’s further submissions
C.CLAUSE 35.2 IS NOT A TIME BAR
17.The Respondent’s adjudication response (at [9]–[14]) repeats its contention that, because the Claimant did not give notice of the access delay within the time in clause 35.2, it has no entitlement to an extension of time for it.
18.Clause 35.2 must be read with clause 35.6, under which the Superintendent:
“may, at any time and for any reason, and whether or not the Contractor has given notice under clause 35.2, extend the Date for Practical Completion.”
19.The Superintendent’s power to extend time whether or not notice was given is inconsistent with the giving of notice being a condition precedent to an extension. A contract that made notice a condition precedent would not, in the next clause, confer a power to extend time without it.
20.The power is to be exercised honestly and fairly, and for the benefit of both parties: Peninsula Balmain Pty Ltd v Abigroup Contractors Pty Ltd [2002] NSWCA 211. Where, as here, the delay was caused by the Principal’s own failure to give access to the western batter on the date required by the Contract (Harlow SD at [14]–[19]; Tabs 8–11), a fair exercise of the power requires the extension to be granted.
21.Nor can the Principal recover liquidated damages for a delay it caused itself. Absent clear words, the Contract does not permit that result: Gaymark Investments Pty Ltd v Walter Construction Group Ltd [1999] NTSC 143. Clause 35 contains no such words.
22.The Adjudicator should therefore determine that the Claimant is entitled to the extension of 19 days claimed, and that the Respondent’s deduction of $83,600.00 as liquidated damages was not open to it.

Part 4 – Extension of time and liquidated damages
4.1The contractual regime
77.The Subcontract fixed the Date for Practical Completion as 14 February 2025 (Subcontract, Item 9; Tab 3). Liquidated damages are payable at $5,000.00 per day for each day by which Practical Completion is not achieved by that date, as extended (Item 16).

“The Subcontractor must, within 7 days after becoming aware of any delay to the progress of the Subcontract Works, give the Contractor written notice of the delay and its cause.”

4.3The Claimant’s notices
82.The Claimant gave written notice of each period of inclement weather within the time required by clause 34.1 (Tabs 57–59).
83.The Claimant gave written notice of the drawing delay on 11 September 2024, 9 days after the delay began (Tab 60). The Respondent acknowledged the notice the following day, and raised no objection to its timing until the Payment Schedule (Tab 61).
4.4The notice requirement in clause 34.1 is not a condition precedent
84.The Respondent contends that, because notice of the drawing delay was given 9 days after the delay began, clause 34.1 disentitles the Claimant to any extension of time for that delay (Payment Schedule, item 7). That contention should be rejected, for three reasons.
85.First, clause 34.1 imposes an obligation to give notice. It does not provide that compliance is a condition of the entitlement to an extension of time under clause 34.2, or that the entitlement is lost if notice is given late.
86.Secondly, where the parties intended a time bar, they said so. Clause 34.3 provides that a claim for delay costs “shall be barred” unless made within 28 days. The absence of those words from clause 34.1 is telling:
(a)the parties are taken to have chosen their words deliberately, and chose words of forfeiture for delay costs only; and
(b)a provision that would deprive the Claimant of an accrued entitlement is construed strictly, and is not extended by implication beyond its terms.
87.Thirdly, clause 34.5 empowers the Respondent to extend time “at any time and whether or not the Subcontractor has claimed an extension”. That power is inconsistent with notice being a condition precedent and, where the Respondent itself caused the delay, must be exercised honestly and fairly: Peninsula Balmain Pty Ltd v Abigroup Contractors Pty Ltd [2002] NSWCA 211. Nor can the Respondent recover liquidated damages for a delay caused by its own late drawings: Gaymark Investments Pty Ltd v Walter Construction Group Ltd [1999] NTSC 143.
88.In any event the Respondent suffered no prejudice. It acknowledged the notice the day after it was given and raised no objection to its timing until the Payment Schedule (Tab 61).
89.The Claimant’s notice of the drawing delay therefore does not affect its entitlement to the extension of time addressed in section 4.5 below.
4.4The extension claimed
84.The Claimant is entitled to an extension of the Date for Practical Completion of 37 days, to 23 March 2025. Practical Completion was achieved on 21 March 2025 (Tab 64).
85.It follows that the Respondent was not entitled to deduct liquidated damages of $185,000.00 (37 days at $5,000.00 per day), and that amount forms part of the adjudicated amount.
4.5Interest
86.Interest is payable on the adjudicated amount under s 73 of the Act from the due date for payment, 30 March 2025.
